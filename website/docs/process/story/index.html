Chapter 1

Who it's about

Jatin shoots, edits and directs. He cuts in DaVinci Resolve and builds his own tools inside Fusion. He gives notes the way an editor does: too slow, hits too soft, it should hold longer. He isn't a developer, and he didn't want a developer's website.

Late in the build, he pointed at the description on the ABOUT page and said it was the context for the whole site, and that anything that didn't match it should change. This is what it says:

Photographer, editor, director from India, and I never stop experimenting. I'd rather burn out chasing what I love than fade away doing what I don't.The ABOUT plate, 8 Nov 2026

That second line is the brief for everything else in this document. Nothing on the site sits still for the sake of it, every screen starts held back, and you are the one who lets it loose.

Chapter 2

Don't make a website

The idea was there before any code. In August he had two things he loved and wanted merged on purpose.

His own JATIN logo animation gave the look: acid yellow, cyan, magenta, purple, green and pink on pure black, shards bursting from the centre, a fine halftone print, a chromatic glitch on the type. A Persona 5 Royal game menu gave the behaviour: one screen, items you pick, sections that slam open over it.

So the site doesn't scroll. It opens like a game: a title sequence, then a menu, then screens that slam open over it. For an editor that's the natural shape. The first impression is a cut, and the visitor's first job is to press something.

Why it works: a scrolling portfolio asks you to read. A menu asks you to choose. Choosing is more fun, and every choice gets an answer on screen: the plate floods with colour, the whole world re-tints, a burst rips through the frame.

Chapter 3

The chain: every screen starts held back

This is the idea that ties the whole site together, and it's his. Chains and breaking out are a metaphor for breaking his own limits. Once it appeared in the intro, he brought it back on every screen. Each one arrives held, strains, and breaks loose, and in almost every case it's your click that breaks it.

  1. Intro

    His name, in zero gravity

    JATIN floats in the dark with slack chains drifting around it, pinned at the four corners. It waits there, under a small blinking line: CLICK ANY BUTTON.

    Your press is the yank. The slack goes in a single frame, the word jolts square with a thud, it strains and trembles, and then the chains snap. The snap is the first frame of the explosion that follows. He asked for it like a game: keep it floating until the user clicks any button.

  2. Projects

    The work is locked inside a machine

    A beige retro computer rises out of the floor, wrapped in a band and an X of chain. It squeezes for a moment and then breaks out with a full-page flash, a shockwave and links flying past you. He asked for it more aggressive, with motion and a flash.

    Then the screen boots and says CLICK ME!! Click, and the camera dives into the glass, where his photos and films live.

  3. Skills

    A break needs a cause

    The first version had the card's chains fall away on their own. His note: the chains just break out of nowhere, but in the reference you can see that the chains break because of the red stripe.

    So it was rebuilt with a cause. There's a dead-still hold, a bolt tears in, and the chains break because something hit them. A break with no cause is just an effect. Here something does it.

  4. Contact

    The phone rings until you pick up

    A flip phone arrives chained shut and ringing, with a comic CLICK IT! beside it. When he saw the first version he corrected how the break should happen: it breaks open from the flip phone opening. The lid is what bursts the chains.

    Then it buzzes like a text, CLICK AGAIN!, and it comes up close to ask what you want to make together.

  5. Menu and About

    After the break, the chain keeps moving

    On the menu one endless chain scrolls along a path he drew. He didn't like that it seemed to come out of nowhere, so now it comes out of the light at the vanishing point, tiny and far, and grows toward you until it runs off the bottom of the frame.

    On ABOUT the same chain loops behind him and past him. Once he's broken out, the chain stops holding anything and turns into motion.

Why it works

Tension gives the release its meaning. His first opening was random sparks on a HUD, and he rejected it as empty and random, no intention. A break only lands if you've watched something held first. The chain gave the intro a reason to exist.

The contrast is the beat. He asked for the float to be slower because it felt a bit too flowy, so the one-frame yank would hit harder. Slow, then sudden. That gap is where the impact lives.

The visitor is the cause. Nothing breaks until you press. Most portfolios ask you to watch. This one makes you the reason he gets free, every time you arrive.

The name survives the break. An early version let JATIN disappear when the chains snapped. His note: after the break I don't want the name to disappear, continue with the chaos. Breaking out doesn't erase who you are. The name takes the hit and stays.

Chapter 4

Timing is the deliverable

He's a motion person, so the motion got the most notes. He judges it the way he'd judge a cut: by feel, frame by frame, and he keeps going until it lands.

The intro runs on a 24fps clock and every shape is redrawn on twos, holding each drawing for two frames, the way hand-drawn animation does. That stepped rhythm is what makes it hit like impact frames instead of sliding like a tween.

The explosion after the snap is a good example of how he decides. It was tried at 2.5 seconds, then 2.0, then 1.5, then back to 2.5, and finally:

Let's actually keep it at 1.85 seconds. The sweet spot.On the intro's burst, after trying four lengths

Chapter 6

Every screen is an object you can hold

None of the sections is a page of text. Each one is a thing: a beige computer you switch on, a handycam that projects him, a chrome trading card you flip, a flip phone that rings. They all come from his references and from his corner of the world: CRTs, floppy drives, Y2K phones, camcorders.

Why it works: his work isn't a grid of thumbnails, it's something you walk up to and turn on. The objects make you curious enough to click, and clicking is how you reach the work.

Every one of them is built inside the single page file with no 3D library, so the whole site can be handed to any host and just work. The computer, the phone and the camera are each drawn by one shader, like a tiny renderer written for that one object.

The phone, in 27 rounds

The flip phone shows how far he'll push one object. Round one was a plain silver clamshell. He called it poorly modeled … plain, no textures, bland and flat and brought two Y2K deco phone references. Over 27 rounds it became lime chrome with the site's ink dots printed into its surface, a keypad drawn the way he sketched it, hinges shaped like his green line, stickers made from the site's own marks, a real hanging chain with a JATIN tag, and a screen that turns into a chat to reach him.

Even the cursor

He wanted the pointer to have its own personality and behaviour. It's a pixel block with weight: it leans when you move it, swaps to a hand over anything clickable with a punch and a glint, and sinks like a key when you click. When you stop, it stops. No trail, because a trail would be the idle flicker he'd already cut.

Chapter 7

Made of his own material

Almost nothing on the site is borrowed. The look comes from his logo video, every heading is set in his own typeface (Cyberway Riders), the photos and films in the computer are his, and the copy is in his voice.

Twice he stopped the site from speaking as someone else. The first time it was the studio he works with:

I don't want you to use BTN for everything. BTN is a separate brand that I work with, and this is my portfolio.2 Nov 2026

The second time it was tone. The skill cards had taglines, and he called them too cheesy. They were rewritten as plain first-person lines, restless like the ABOUT plate:

The camera is the right metaphor for someone who shoots, edits and directs: he's the subject and the one behind the lens.

Chapter 8

How he works

Reading back through more than a hundred rounds of notes, the same habits show up again and again. Together they're his process.

He starts from a reference

Almost every piece began with something he brought: pop-art title intros, a rendered retro computer, Y2K deco phones, a psychedelic eye, a camcorder projecting light. Then the ask is to take it apart and rebuild it in his language.

“analyze every detail of the reference and pay attention to them”

He draws on the screen

When words aren't enough he marks up a screenshot: red lines for a chain's path, green for a hinge's shape, a box where a button should go, scribbles over an area that should be filled.

“I drew with a green line so it can be easier for you to follow”

He decides by seeing

He'll ask for something, watch it, and change his mind. Text on the ABOUT banners went in and out twice. Grabbing the camera was added, then removed. He doesn't treat that as wasted work. Seeing it is how he finds out.

“actually just remove the grabbing and go back to the previous state”

He notices the small things

A faint dashed box around a sticker, a single frame of gap after a beam, CLICK ME reading wrong in a display face, a chain link that spikes past a letter. Small flaws break the illusion, so they get fixed.

“why is there extra chain spiking up around the logo?”

He pushes for more, then pulls back

The living wedge went not too harsh, then too subtle, more motion and contrast, then wider, flowy, add a small dither. He finds the edge by going past it.

“not too heavy, not too subtle”

He keeps it simple to use

For all the noise on screen, the controls stay simple. One BACK button, top-left on every page. Hints that say TAP on a phone and CLICK on a laptop. No EXIT, no second way out.

“that's better and simple”

Chapter 9

Built for the person on the other side

Loud doesn't have to mean hard to use. Twice before launch he had five simulated first-time visitors walk the site: an impatient skimmer, a skeptical recruiter, someone on a phone with slow 3G, a non-native English reader and someone trying to break it. Each went from the intro to seeing one of his photos full size or sending him a message, and every snag was fixed before they went back in.

What came out of it: the browser's Back button climbs the computer one level at a time, presses during an entrance are kept instead of thrown away, the words change for touch screens, and the contact chat always ends somewhere real, with your message in his inbox.

The world is loud so the work feels alive. The controls are plain so nobody gets lost on the way to it.

Chapter 10

Where it stands

The site went live on 8 November at justjatin.thotahub.in, replacing the shorter version he'd built for a Vogue application, which went straight to his work. Every menu screen is finished. What's left is performance work so the heavier screens run smoothly on more machines, and a few pieces he's still adding: his exported logo in place of the font version, and material for the BTN folder.

The site works the way he describes himself. It starts held back, it waits for a push, and when it breaks loose it doesn't hold back.

I'd rather burn out chasing what I love than fade away doing what I don't.Jatin
